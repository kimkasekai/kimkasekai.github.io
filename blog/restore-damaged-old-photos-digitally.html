How to Digitally Restore Damaged Old Photos Using Free Online Tools

Published: July 8, 2026

← Back to Blog

Faded grandparents' portraits, cracked sepia prints, photos with water stains from a leaky basement — these family treasures can usually be rescued without expensive professional services. A scanner and a free browser-based editor are all you need.

Step One: Protect the Original

Before anything else, handle the physical print carefully. Never attempt to clean, bend, or unstick an old photo — fragile emulsion flakes off easily. If the photo is mounted in an album and refuses to lift, photograph it in place rather than forcing it out. Your restoration work happens on the digital copy only; the original stays exactly as it is, stored flat in a cool, dry place away from sunlight.

Scan It Right the First Time

The quality of your scan determines the ceiling of what restoration can achieve:

  • Use at least 600 DPI. Higher resolution captures fine detail and gives you room to work on small defects. For tiny wallet-sized originals, go up to 1200 DPI.
  • Scan in color even for black-and-white photos. Sepia tones and age-related yellowing carry information that helps later correction stages.
  • Wipe the scanner glass with a microfiber cloth first — dust specks become permanent spots otherwise.
  • Save as TIFF or PNG, never JPEG, to avoid compression artifacts piling onto existing damage.

Repair Tears, Scratches, and Missing Corners

Open your scan in an online editor and zoom to 100% or higher to map every defect. Then repair systematically:

  1. Dust and light scratches: Run a spot-healing or dust-removal pass over smooth areas like skies and backgrounds.
  2. Tears and creases: Use the clone stamp, sampling texture from adjacent undamaged areas and painting across the break in short strokes.
  3. Missing chunks: Select the gap and apply content-aware fill so the software rebuilds plausible detail from surrounding pixels.
  4. Facial damage: Slow down here. Eyes, mouths, and skin need gentle healing-brush work — these features are what viewers notice first.

Correct Fading and Color Shifts

Old prints rarely fade evenly. Fix this in two moves:

  • Levels or curves adjustment: Pull the black point inward until shadows are truly dark, and the white point inward until highlights are bright. This single step reverses most of the washed-out look.
  • Color balance correction: Old photos drift toward yellow or magenta. Reduce those channels slightly, or use an auto white-balance tool targeting a neutral area of the image.

For black-and-white restorations, convert fully to grayscale after fixing tones — this eliminates stubborn yellow staining in one click while keeping all your tonal corrections intact.

Where AI Restoration Fits In

Modern AI-powered tools can sharpen blur, reconstruct faces, and remove noise impressively well, and several free online editors include them. They work best as a finishing step after manual repairs. Two cautions: AI face reconstruction sometimes invents features that never existed, so compare results against the original before accepting them; and heavily stylized results may no longer feel like the person you remember. When accuracy matters more than polish, keep AI adjustments subtle.

Preserve Everything You Make

Keep three files: the untouched original scan, your working file with layers if the editor supports them, and a final JPEG for easy sharing. Back them up to cloud storage plus an external drive. Family members will want copies — and unlike paper, digital files can be duplicated endlessly without degrading.

Restoration rewards patience. Budget an evening per photo, save often, and compare against the original regularly. The moment a long-lost relative's face emerges clean and clear makes every minute worthwhile.
